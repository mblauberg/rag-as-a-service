Chapter 2: Literature Review and Theoretical Foundations
2.1 Conceptual Foundations of Romance Fraud
2.1.1 Process Models of Romance Fraud Progression
Whitty's (2013) stage-based model of online dating romance fraud represents the foundational conceptual framework in this domain. The "Scammers' Persuasive Technique Model" proposes sequential progression through grooming, sting, and continuation phases, with each stage characterised by distinct communicative tactics. During grooming, perpetrators rapidly escalate intimacy through frequent declarations of affection, whilst simultaneously establishing private communication channels separate from monitored platforms. The sting phase introduces financial requests, typically framed as urgent crises or positioned as minor favours through foot-in-the-door techniques. Continuation involves repeated exploitation through fabricated emergencies and door-in-the-face negotiation tactics, wherein extreme requests precede more moderate (yet still substantial) demands.
Whitty's model incorporates the "near-win" phenomenon from gambling psychology, wherein intermittent rewards and imminent promises maintain victim engagement despite accumulating losses. This mechanism purportedly explains persistence in the face of contradictory evidence and post-scam re-victimisation patterns.
Critical evaluation: Whilst Whitty's model enjoys widespread citation and provides useful organisational structure, several limitations warrant scrutiny. First, the empirical basis derives primarily from victim interviews (n=20 in the foundational study), raising questions about generalisability across fraud typologies and cultural contexts. The model may impose artificial discretisation on what could be continuous processes, particularly given that modern fraud operations demonstrate increasing sophistication and variation. Second, the stage boundaries lack operationalised definitions that would permit reliable classification—at what point does "grooming" transition to "sting"? Such ambiguity complicates both theoretical testing and practical application. Third, the model reflects predominantly Western, heterosexual relationship norms; whether it captures fraud dynamics in collectivist cultures, same-sex relationships, or non-romantic exploitation remains unexamined. Finally, selection bias likely operates: victims who report fraud may represent particularly egregious or obvious cases, whilst successful frauds maintaining subtler approaches escape documentation.
Subsequent research has proposed refinements. Oak et al.'s (2025) analysis of "pig-butchering" cryptocurrency scams identified extended financial grooming phases (3-11 months) preceding investment suggestions, representing elaboration rather than contradiction of Whitty's framework. However, Cross et al.'s (2018) identification of "two-layer scams" combining professional and romantic deception suggests typological diversity that simple stage models may inadequately capture.
2.1.2 Trust Formation in Mediated Relationships
Romance fraud exploits computer-mediated communication (CMC) affordances to accelerate trust formation beyond face-to-face relationship norms. Perpetrators employ what Whitty and Buchanan (2012) term "love bombing"—overwhelming targets with affection, constant communication, and future-oriented discourse. Quantitative analysis indicates perpetrators often declare love within two weeks of initial contact, a temporal acceleration markedly deviant from typical relationship development.
Several mechanisms facilitate rapid trust establishment. First, hyperpersonal communication theory (Walther, 1996) suggests CMC enables idealised self-presentation and intensified relationships through selective disclosure and asynchronous reflection. Perpetrators exploit this by crafting personas precisely calibrated to target preferences whilst maintaining continuous communication that establishes routinised companionship. Second, perpetrators strategically create exclusivity and secrecy, framing relationships as uniquely intimate whilst isolating targets from social networks that might provide reality checks. Third, future planning and idealisation generate psychological commitment through detailed elaboration of shared prospects (marriage, retirement, relocation).
Critical evaluation: The literature on trust formation in romance fraud demonstrates several conceptual and methodological limitations. Much research relies on retrospective victim accounts, which suffer from hindsight bias—victims reconstruct relationship progression with knowledge of fraudulent outcome, potentially distorting recall of early interactions. What appear as obvious manipulation tactics in retrospect may have seemed less anomalous in real time. Additionally, the literature inadequately distinguishes romance fraud dynamics from legitimate accelerated online relationships. Some genuine long-distance relationships do exhibit rapid emotional escalation, frequent communication, and extended pre-meeting periods. Without comparison to matched legitimate relationships, the specificity of proposed indicators remains unclear.
Furthermore, trust formation research inadequately addresses cultural variation. Communication norms regarding emotional expression, relationship pacing, and privacy vary substantially across cultures. Indicators calibrated to Western (particularly Anglo-American) communication styles may generate false positives in collectivist cultures where formal politeness or rapid familial integration carry different significance.
The theoretical grounding also warrants scrutiny. Whilst hyperpersonal theory provides useful explanatory power, it was developed to explain positive CMC outcomes, not manipulative exploitation. Whether the mechanisms operate identically in genuine versus fraudulent contexts—or whether perpetrators deliberately engineer hyperpersonal dynamics—remains empirically unresolved.
2.1.3 Platform Affordances and Fraud Ecology
Communicative platforms shape fraud dynamics through technical features, moderation policies, and user expectations. Identity verification mechanisms, message persistence, contact friction, and content moderation create varying opportunity structures and constraint patterns across platforms.
Dating platforms with profile-based matching enable perpetrators to deploy fabricated identities but impose discovery risks through user reporting and algorithmic detection. Consequently, rapid migration to unmoderated channels (WhatsApp, email, Telegram) represents common behavioural adaptation. Professional networks (LinkedIn) afford authority signalling through credentials and recommendations, supporting impersonation of high-status professions (doctors, military officers, executives). Social media platforms enable preliminary reconnaissance, whereby perpetrators mine personal information from public profiles to calibrate approach strategies and demonstrate apparent commonalities.
Message persistence differentially affects fraud dynamics. Persistent channels enable targets to re-read communications, reinforcing emotional bonds, but create evidentiary trails. Ephemeral messaging potentially erases incriminating payment instructions but disrupts the sustained narrative construction central to grooming. Encryption prevents platform-level content moderation but shields both criminal activity and user privacy.
Critical evaluation: Platform affordance analyses in existing literature remain largely descriptive rather than theoretically developed. Gibson's (1979) ecological psychology or Norman's (1988) design theory could provide stronger explanatory frameworks, generating predictions about how affordance interactions shape behavioural adaptations. For instance, affordance theory predicts perpetrators should gravitate toward platforms optimising trust signalling whilst minimising detection risk—a testable proposition requiring systematic platform comparison.
Additionally, the literature exhibits temporal lag. Many analyses reflect platform features from 2015-2020, predating significant changes in verification requirements, AI-powered moderation, and end-to-end encryption adoption. Platform affordances evolve; analyses that fail to track these changes risk obsolescence.
Cross-platform fraud sequences receive insufficient attention. Modern fraud operations span multiple platforms (dating app introduction → messaging app grooming → video platform face-to-face simulation → payment app transactions), yet analyses typically examine single platforms in isolation. Understanding multi-platform trajectories may reveal vulnerabilities in current platform-specific countermeasures.
2.1.4 Evolution and Industrialisation of Fraud Operations
Romance fraud has transformed from individualised opportunistic crime to industrialised organised operations. Contemporary fraud enterprises operate call-centre-like facilities, particularly in West Africa and Southeast Asia, wherein operatives manage multiple concurrent relationships using standardised scripts and management oversight. Some operations involve coerced labour, particularly in Southeast Asian compounds linked to human trafficking.
Scripted approaches enable rapid operator training and consistency but introduce stylometric vulnerabilities. Law enforcement seizures have revealed detailed playbooks containing message templates for each relationship stage, including specific phrasings for wrong-number introductions, romantic escalation, and investment pitches. Script standardisation means victims across continents report eerily similar experiences and identical phrases.
Technological integration represents an emerging frontier. Reports document experimentation with large language models to automate initial conversation phases, enabling parallel relationship management at unprecedented scale. AI-generated photographs circumvent reverse image searches, whilst voice cloning and real-time deepfakes address video verification requests. The "pig-butchering" variant exemplifies sophistication, combining romance and investment fraud through fake trading platforms demonstrating fabricated profits.
Critical evaluation: Claims regarding industrialisation and AI integration require careful scrutiny. Much evidence derives from law enforcement announcements and journalism rather than peer-reviewed research. The actual prevalence of AI-assisted fraud versus traditional methods remains unknown. Reporting bias likely operates: novel AI-enabled fraud attracts media attention disproportionate to its frequency, whilst conventional frauds continue unreported.
The industrialisation narrative also oversimplifies. Whilst organised operations exist, evidence suggests a spectrum from individual opportunists to sophisticated syndicates. Treating romance fraud as monolithic obscures potentially important tactical and scale variations that may require different countermeasures.
Furthermore, the cat-and-mouse dynamic between fraud innovation and detection creates moving target challenges. Detection systems optimised for current tactics may rapidly become obsolete as perpetrators adapt. Yet the literature inadequately addresses adaptive adversarial dynamics, treating fraud tactics as static rather than engaged in co-evolution with defensive measures.
2.2 Conversational and Linguistic Indicators
2.2.1 Cross-Platform Indicator Patterns
Despite platform-specific variations, certain conversational patterns recur across romance fraud contexts. These represent potential discriminative features, though each requires critical evaluation regarding reliability, specificity, and cultural generalisability.
Migration intent: Requests to move conversations off initial contact platforms to private channels appear with high frequency in documented fraud cases. Dating platforms and social media typically see migration requests within initial exchanges, framed as desiring privacy or platform inconvenience. This behaviour serves multiple perpetrator objectives: avoiding platform moderation, establishing intimate private space, and reducing victim exposure to warning systems or peer reality checks.
Temporal acceleration: Romance fraud communications exhibit compressed relationship timelines relative to normative development. Expressions of love, future planning (marriage, children, relocation), and exclusive commitment occur within days or weeks rather than months. This acceleration purportedly creates psychological investment before victims develop scepticism.
Generic and templated language: Perpetrators frequently employ non-personalised communication—compliments applicable to anyone ("you have a beautiful soul"), vague biographical details resistant to verification, and scripted declarations of affection. Suarez-Tangil et al. (2019) found fraud profiles contained significantly longer, more flowery descriptions than genuine profiles, patterns potentially extending to messages.
Financial references and crisis narratives: Eventually, conversations introduce financial dimensions through crisis framing (medical emergencies, legal troubles, business complications) or investment opportunities (cryptocurrency, real estate, trading platforms). Requests may follow foot-in-the-door progression (small favours escalating) or present immediately as significant sums. Perpetrators frequently couple requests with repayment promises and frame financial compliance as trust demonstration.
Urgency and obligation language: Communications surrounding financial requests employ pressure tactics—time constraints ("If we don't act by tomorrow"), sole dependency ("You're the only one who can help"), and trust tests ("Don't you trust me?"). This linguistic coercion transforms reasonable caution into relationship threat.
Consistency failures: Retrospective analysis often reveals contradictions—age discrepancies, location changes, biographical inconsistencies. However, these typically emerge only through systematic review; real-time detection proves challenging.
Meeting avoidance: Despite relationship intensity, physical meetings perpetually defer through escalating excuses (visa problems, work emergencies, health crises, travel restrictions). Video call requests similarly encounter technical difficulties or scheduling conflicts.
Critical evaluation: The evidentiary basis for these indicators exhibits substantial heterogeneity and limitations:
Selection and reporting bias: Most studies analyse confirmed fraud cases, often from victim support services or law enforcement. This sampling strategy inevitably over-represents obvious, unsuccessful frauds whilst missing sophisticated operations that evade detection or reporting. Consequently, indicator prevalence estimates likely suffer systematic inflation. The claim that "almost 100% of confirmed cases" exhibit off-platform migration may simply reflect that cases lacking this obvious signal go unconfirmed.
Absence of matched controls: Few studies employ adequate comparison groups. Without systematic analysis of legitimate accelerated online relationships, whirlwind romances, and cross-cultural courtship norms, the specificity of proposed indicators remains speculative. Some genuine relationships do exhibit rapid emotional progression, extensive communication, and prolonged pre-meeting periods due to distance or circumstances.
Cultural universality assumptions: Indicator sets derived predominantly from Western (particularly UK, US, Australia) contexts may lack cross-cultural validity. Emotional expression norms, relationship pacing expectations, and privacy conventions vary substantially. Direct translation of Western-derived indicators to other cultural contexts risks both false positives and false negatives.
Temporal stability concerns: Perpetrator tactics evolve in response to awareness and countermeasures. Indicators documented in 2015 may poorly predict 2025 fraud. For instance, early research emphasised grammatical errors and spelling mistakes as indicators; contemporary fraud operations employ AI-powered grammar correction, rendering this historical indicator obsolete.
Quantitative imprecision: Much literature employs vague frequency descriptors ("often", "commonly", "typically") without quantitative estimates or confidence intervals. Claims that perpetrators "often profess love within two weeks" lack specification of prevalence (40%? 90%?), sample characteristics, or statistical uncertainty. This imprecision limits indicator operationalisation.
Confounding with base rates: Even high-sensitivity indicators prove practically problematic at realistic base rates. If romance fraud affects 1% of online daters, an indicator with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity yields only 16% positive predictive value—84% of positive detections prove false alarms. The literature inadequately addresses base rate implications for deployment feasibility.
2.2.2 Platform-Specific Tactical Variations
Whilst cross-platform patterns exist, perpetrators adapt tactics to platform affordances:
Dating platforms (Tinder, Bumble, Hinge): Opening messages demonstrate lower personalisation than genuine users. Profile inconsistencies (education claims contradicting literacy) may emerge through dialogue. Rapid off-platform migration requests occur early (often <10 messages).
Messaging platforms (WhatsApp, Telegram): Communication establishes daily rituals (morning/evening messages) creating routinised companionship. Financial discussions follow extended grooming, particularly in pig-butchering variants where perpetrators gradually introduce investment topics (3-11 months in Oak et al.'s analysis). Exclusivity framing intensifies.
Social media (Instagram, Facebook): Initial contact often occurs through public interactions (post comments, photo likes) before private messaging. Perpetrators reference target's visible content, creating false commonality impressions. Profile reconnaissance enables tailored approach.
Professional networks (LinkedIn): Business-to-personal pivots occur, wherein professional networking gradually incorporates personal elements. Authority signalling through credentials, recommendations, and professional achievement narratives establishes trust. High-status impersonations (executives, consultants, military officers) predominate.
Critical evaluation: Platform-specific analyses suffer from several limitations. First, they fragment understanding of multi-platform fraud sequences, which may represent dominant contemporary pattern. Second, platform taxonomies reflect commercial boundaries rather than affordance structures—grouping by actual affordance dimensions (identity persistence, moderation intensity, message ephemerality) might yield more theoretically productive insights. Third, insufficient attention addresses within-platform heterogeneity; Instagram direct messages differ substantially from Instagram comments, yet analyses often treat platforms monolithically.
2.2.3 Theoretical Frameworks for Linguistic Analysis
Psycholinguistic approaches: Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) and similar lexicon-based tools enable systematic quantification of psychological language dimensions including affect, social orientation, cognitive processing, temporal focus, and authenticity. LIWC-22 provides hierarchical category structure (e.g., "sadness" as subcategory of "negative emotion") with expanded coverage of contemporary language including internet slang.
Applied to romance fraud, psycholinguistic analysis might reveal distinctive patterns in pronoun usage (reduced first-person singular potentially indicating distancing), emotional lexicon (positive emotion prevalence during grooming; anxiety during crisis introduction), temporal orientation (future-focused language during idealisation; present-focused during requests), or cognitive complexity markers (reduced insight terms if perpetrators avoid introspection).
Critical evaluation: Psycholinguistic tools exhibit known limitations particularly relevant to fraud detection contexts:
Context insensitivity: LIWC operates through word counting without semantic understanding. The sentence "I don't love you" contains positive emotion lexicon ("love") but conveys negative meaning. Whilst LIWC-22 incorporates some contextual handling, fundamental limitations persist.
Category reliability concerns: Hunter and Grant (2025) documented substantial LIWC classification errors, with precision and recall around 50% for some categories, indicating frequent misattribution or omission. Such unreliability introduces measurement error that may obscure genuine effects or generate spurious patterns.
Domain specificity: LIWC lexicons derive from general language corpora and may inadequately capture domain-specific terminology. Romance and financial discourse employ specialised vocabulary requiring domain-adapted lexicons.
Modest effect sizes: Even where psycholinguistic differences exist between deceptive and truthful communication, effect sizes typically prove small (d < 0.3), limiting practical discriminability. DePaulo et al.'s (2003) meta-analysis of deception cues found weak average effects across all linguistic markers.
Deception detection literature: Decades of deception research identify potential linguistic markers: reduced self-reference pronouns (distancing), increased negative emotion terms (guilt or stress), narrative inconsistencies, hedging or hesitation markers, and reduced cognitive complexity terms. However, meta-analyses consistently demonstrate small, unstable effects highly dependent on context, deception type, and deceiver characteristics.
Critical evaluation: Romance fraud represents prolonged, premeditated, practiced deception by potentially sociopathic individuals managing multiple concurrent deceptions. Traditional deception cues derived from laboratory studies of unpracticed lies told by normal populations may not transfer. Skilled deceivers demonstrating low guilt and high practice may exhibit minimal cognitive load effects. Furthermore, deception cue research exhibits publication bias favouring positive findings; the true effect distribution may be even weaker than published estimates suggest.
Stylometric and template detection: Standardised fraud scripts create potential stylometric vulnerabilities through repeated distinctive phrases, unusual collocations, or abnormal statistical distributions. Suarez-Tangil et al. (2019) achieved 97% accuracy detecting fraud profiles through combining various textual features.
Critical evaluation: Template detection faces adversarial adaptation challenges. Awareness of stylometric detection incentivises script variation, paraphrasing, and AI-generated text diversity. Furthermore, template detection requires substantial fraud corpora for training, creating cold-start problems for novel fraud variants. The reported 97% accuracy likely reflects optimal conditions (same-platform, same-period data) and may degrade substantially in operational deployment across platforms and time.
2.3 Measurement and Annotation Paradigms
2.3.1 Challenges in Constructing Fraud Indicators
Several fundamental challenges complicate romance fraud indicator development:
Definitional ambiguity: What constitutes "romance fraud" lacks consensus. Some definitions require financial loss; others include attempted fraud or psychological manipulation without monetary component. Relationship fraud, sweetheart scam, confidence trick, and catfishing represent overlapping but non-identical concepts. Definitional inconsistency across studies complicates evidence synthesis.
Ground truth limitations: Fraud confirmation typically requires victim realisation and reporting, criminal investigation, or financial loss documentation. However, many frauds remain unrecognised or unreported due to embarrassment, hope, or gradual realisation. This creates unverified negative cases (actual frauds misclassified as legitimate) alongside confirmed positives, contaminating training data.
Retrospective reconstruction bias: Most fraud data derives from post-discovery interviews or message archives. Victims reconstruct narratives with knowledge of outcomes, potentially distorting recall of early interactions. What appear as obvious manipulation tactics retrospectively may have seemed ambiguous in real time.
Comparison group construction: Valid indicator development requires appropriate control groups—legitimate relationships exhibiting comparable characteristics (online-initiated, long-distance, accelerated). Few studies employ such matched comparisons, instead comparing fraud cases to unselected relationship norms, potentially confounding fraud-specific patterns with online relationship characteristics generally.
Annotation reliability: Indicators requiring subjective interpretation (Is this compliment "excessive"? Does this disclosure seem "inconsistent"?) suffer inter-rater reliability challenges. Even seemingly objective indicators like "professed love" require judgment regarding whether a statement qualifies.
2.3.2 Event Annotation Schemas
Several approaches exist for structuring conversation annotation:
Speech act classification: Categorising utterances by illocutionary force (compliment, request, promise, disclosure, question). Provides fine-grained characterisation but requires trained annotators and suffers reliability challenges with indirect speech acts.
Stage-based annotation: Classifying entire conversations or segments into fraud progression stages (grooming, financial approach, sting, continuation). Provides coarse organisational structure but struggles with unclear boundaries and conversations exhibiting multiple simultaneous stages.
Event-based annotation: Identifying specific events (platform migration request, financial mention, meeting cancellation, inconsistency) regardless of stage. Provides moderate granularity with potentially higher reliability for explicit events, though struggles with implicit or ambiguous instances.
Dimensional rating: Rating conversations on continuous dimensions (intimacy level, pressure intensity, commitment speed). Enables nuanced characterisation but requires well-anchored scales and careful attention to rater calibration.
Critical evaluation: No annotation approach escapes trade-offs between granularity, reliability, and theoretical alignment. Speech act schemes provide rich detail but require substantial annotation expertise and time investment. Stage-based approaches impose strong theoretical commitments potentially misrepresenting continuous processes. Event-based schemes achieve reliability for explicit events but struggle with borderline cases. Dimensional approaches demand extensive rater training for scale anchoring.
Crucially, annotation schema selection should follow pilot empirical evaluation rather than pure theoretical prescription. Bottom-up examination of natural category distinctions that annotators reliably perceive should inform top-down theoretical category imposition. Few studies report such iterative schema development.
2.3.3 Inter-Rater Reliability Considerations
Annotation reliability fundamentally constrains detection system performance—systems cannot exceed the consistency of their training labels. Adequate reliability assessment requires:
Multiple metrics: Cohen's κ for two raters; Fleiss' κ or Krippendorff's α for multiple raters. Agreement percentage alone proves insufficient as it includes chance agreement.
Category-specific evaluation: Aggregate reliability may mask category-level variation. Some events (explicit financial requests) likely exhibit high reliability, whilst others (subtle isolation attempts) may prove unreliable.
Disagreement analysis: Examining disagreement patterns reveals whether errors are random or systematic, whether certain contexts produce consistent disagreements, and whether additional annotator training could resolve issues.
Sufficient sample size: Reliability estimation requires adequate sampling. Reliability assessments based on <50 instances per category risk substantial sampling error.
Adjudication documentation: When disagreements require resolution, documentation of adjudication rationale enables schema refinement and reveals persistent ambiguities.
Typical reliability standards suggest κ ≥ 0.70 for acceptable agreement, κ ≥ 0.80 for strong agreement. However, standards should reflect domain characteristics—categories with severe class imbalance require adjusted interpretation as κ is sensitive to prevalence.
2.4 Technical Foundations: Language Models and Context Limitations
2.4.1 Transformer Architectures and Long-Context Challenges
Transformer-based language models have achieved state-of-the-art performance across diverse natural language processing tasks through self-attention mechanisms enabling global context modelling. However, several well-documented limitations constrain their application to long-document or extended-dialogue analysis:
Positional biases: Liu et al. (2023) demonstrated that even models explicitly extended for long contexts exhibit significant positional bias, disproportionately utilising information at sequence beginnings and endings whilst under-attending to middle content. This "lost in the middle" phenomenon manifests as substantially degraded performance on tasks requiring middle-position information access, with performance declining as relevant information shifts toward sequence centres.
Attention allocation pathologies: Self-attention mechanisms distribute computational resources across all tokens, potentially diluting focus when critical signals comprise small fractions of large contexts. In extended dialogues containing hundreds of exchanges, salient indicators may receive insufficient attention weight, particularly when surrounded by routine conversational filler.
Computational scaling: Standard transformer self-attention exhibits quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, creating memory and computational bottlenecks as context extends. Whilst recent efficient attention variants (sparse attention, linear attention) mitigate this, they introduce approximation errors that may impact performance on tasks requiring precise long-range dependency modelling.
Context window limitations: Most publicly available pre-trained models support maximum context lengths of 2,048-8,192 tokens. Whilst some models now advertise 16K-100K token windows, effective utilisation of extended contexts remains challenging. Extended context often requires specific training objectives, and models may fail to leverage additional context even when technically within their window.
Critical evaluation: Several important qualifications temper these concerns:
First, transformer limitations represent active research areas with rapid progress. Recent architectures (Mamba, RWKV, extended Transformer-XL variants) demonstrate improved long-context handling. Treating current limitations as immutable constraints risks premature architectural commitment when superior alternatives may emerge.
Second, limitations' severity depends on task characteristics. Positional bias problematises tasks requiring specific middle-content access, but tasks leveraging distributional patterns across entire contexts may prove less vulnerable.
Third, empirical severity remains unclear for specific applications. The degree to which positional bias affects romance fraud detection, wherein indicators may cluster temporally (early grooming, late financial requests), requires empirical assessment rather than assumption.
Fourth, architectural limitations should not deterministically prescribe system design. Engineering solutions (chunking, hierarchical processing, retrieval augmentation) can address limitations, but whether such complexity improves performance versus simpler approaches requires empirical validation.
2.4.2 Alternative Architecture Considerations
Several architectural alternatives to standard transformers exist for long-context processing:
Recurrent architectures: LSTMs and GRUs process sequences incrementally, maintaining fixed-size hidden states encoding conversation history. This enables unlimited sequence length but struggles to maintain relevant information over extended contexts due to the vanishing gradient problem.
Memory-augmented networks: Neural Turing Machines and Differentiable Neural Computers incorporate external memory structures enabling explicit storage and retrieval of past information. These architectures theoretically handle unlimited context but face training difficulties and limited adoption in large-scale language modelling.
State-space models: Recent architectures like Mamba employ state-space formulations achieving linear computational complexity whilst maintaining competitive performance with transformers. These represent promising alternatives for long-sequence processing but remain relatively unexplored in dialogue applications.
Hybrid approaches: Combining transformers for local context with recurrent or memory mechanisms for long-range dependencies represents another design pattern.
Critical evaluation: Each alternative exhibits distinct trade-offs regarding computational efficiency, modelling capacity, training stability, and empirical performance. Furthermore, the relative absence of large-scale pre-trained models for alternative architectures creates practical barriers—transformers benefit from extensive pre-training investment that alternatives lack. Whether alternative architectures' theoretical advantages translate to superior performance on specific tasks remains primarily empirical question.
2.5 Context Management Strategies for Extended Dialogues
2.5.1 Sliding Window Approaches
The most straightforward context management strategy maintains fixed-size windows over dialogue history, retaining only the K most recent tokens or turns. As new content arrives, oldest content drops from context.
Advantages: Computational simplicity, fixed memory requirements, and straightforward implementation.
Disadvantages: Historical information loss, inability to reference early conversation elements, and potential failure to detect patterns requiring long-range context integration.
Empirical performance: Sliding windows have demonstrated success for tasks where local context suffices but prove inadequate when critical information disperses across extended timescales. For dialogue systems, sliding windows risk forgetting early conversational commitments, biographical details, or relationship establishment patterns potentially relevant for fraud assessment.
2.5.2 Summarisation and Compression
Summarisation approaches periodically compress historical context into condensed representations—either natural language summaries or learned compressed embeddings—retaining these alongside recent verbatim context.
Advantages: Retains historical information footprint whilst controlling computational costs. Enables referencing distant content without maintaining complete history.
Disadvantages: Compression necessarily discards information, with uncertain implications for subtle pattern detection. Summarisation quality depends on summariser capabilities; automatic summarisation may introduce errors or omit precisely those subtle cues most relevant for fraud detection. Abstract summarisation (generating novel text) risks introducing information not present in original, whilst extractive summarisation (selecting existing sentences) may miss distributional patterns across non-selected content.
Empirical performance: Summarisation has achieved success in question-answering and information-seeking dialogues where explicit content retrieval suffices. However, tasks requiring stylistic analysis, subtle linguistic cues, or pattern detection across numerous small signals may suffer from summarisation's information bottleneck.
2.5.3 Retrieval-Augmented Generation
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) maintains conversation history in searchable memory, dynamically retrieving relevant segments when processing current context. Retrieved segments augment input to the language model, effectively providing targeted historical context without processing complete history.
Advantages: Selective context provision enables surfacing relevant historical content whilst controlling input size. Retrieval can target specific semantic content, structural patterns, or metadata-defined segments. Scales to arbitrarily long conversations as retrieval cost typically sublinear with history length.
Disadvantages: Retrieval quality critically determines performance—failures to retrieve relevant content or retrieval of irrelevant material degrade results. Retrieval query formulation proves challenging for abstract tasks lacking explicit information needs. Dense retrieval requires semantic similarity alignment between queries and relevant content, which may fail when relevant connections are subtle or abstract.
Empirical performance: RAG has demonstrated substantial improvements over baseline models for knowledge-intensive tasks, particularly question-answering where queries naturally specify retrieval targets. Applications to tasks with implicit or abstract retrieval needs remain less explored. Retrieval strategies for fraud detection, where "relevance" may involve subtle pattern matches or contradictions rather than explicit content similarity, require careful design.
2.5.4 Hierarchical and Structured Memory Systems
Hierarchical approaches decompose memory into multiple levels or structures, such as short-term verbatim memory of recent context, long-term episodic memory of significant events, and semantic memory of extracted patterns or facts. Processing proceeds through coordinated access across memory levels.
Advantages: Mirrors human memory organisation, potentially enabling more sophisticated context integration. Enables explicit representation of important events, extracted facts, or structured knowledge alongside raw conversation text. Different retrieval strategies can target different memory types based on processing needs.
Disadvantages: Architectural complexity increases substantially. Requires design decisions regarding what constitutes "significant" events meriting episodic storage, how to extract and represent semantic knowledge, and how to coordinate across memory types. Risks introducing multiple points of failure where suboptimal design choices degrade overall performance.
Empirical performance: Hierarchical memory systems have demonstrated success in cognitive architectures and some dialogue systems, particularly long-term conversational agents tracking user preferences and conversation history across sessions. Application specifically to fraud detection or other analytical tasks remains limited, with unclear empirical validation.
Critical evaluation of context management strategies: Several overarching considerations bear on strategy selection:
First, optimal strategies likely depend on task characteristics in ways that preclude universal recommendations. Tasks with strong recency biases may benefit from sliding windows; tasks requiring specific fact retrieval may benefit from RAG; tasks requiring integration of subtle distributional patterns across entire histories may require full-context processing or sophisticated compression.
Second, engineering complexity exhibits non-linear costs. Sophisticated context management introduces additional failure modes, hyperparameters requiring tuning, and computational overhead beyond core model inference. These costs may outweigh performance benefits, particularly for tasks achievable through simpler approaches.
Third, empirical validation on specific tasks should drive strategy selection rather than architectural intuitions. Different context management approaches embody different implicit assumptions about where relevant information resides and how it should be accessed. Without empirical testing, these assumptions remain speculative.
Fourth, adversarial considerations complicate context management design for fraud detection. If perpetrators learn that systems emphasise recent context, they may strategically position deceptive elements earlier. If systems rely on explicit markers for episodic memory, perpetrators may avoid clear markers. Context management strategies robust to adversarial adaptation require particular attention.
2.6 Practical Constraints and Deployment Considerations
2.6.1 Base Rate Problems and Positive Predictive Value
Romance fraud prevalence estimates vary but consistently indicate low base rates relative to overall online relationship formation. UK Finance (2023) reported approximately 8,000 romance fraud cases among tens of millions of online dating users in the UK, suggesting base rates below 0.1%. Even higher estimates (accounting for underreporting) likely remain below 1%.
Low base rates create severe challenges for positive predictive value (PPV), the proportion of positive classifications that are correct:

At 0.5% fraud prevalence, a detector with 95% sensitivity and 95% specificity achieves only 9% PPV—91% of alerts prove false. Achieving 50% PPV at this base rate requires 99.5% specificity; achieving 90% PPV requires 99.95% specificity.
Implications: Base rate mathematics fundamentally constrains deployment feasibility. Acceptable false positive rates depend on consequences—users may tolerate occasional false warnings for high-stakes fraud, but frequent false alarms create warning fatigue and system abandonment. This tension necessitates either exceptional specificity (approaching levels rarely achieved in machine learning) or careful consideration of how to present uncertain warnings without generating fatigue.
Some strategies for managing base rate challenges:
Staged classification: Multi-stage systems wherein an initial high-sensitivity, moderate-specificity filter identifies candidates for more intensive (and specific) analysis can improve overall precision.
Risk scoring rather than binary classification: Presenting continuous risk scores rather than binary fraud/legitimate classifications enables users to calibrate their own thresholds and permits different interventions at different risk levels.
Selective deployment: Restricting detection to higher-risk contexts (users reporting concerns, conversations exhibiting preliminary warning signs, users with vulnerability indicators) increases effective base rates among screened population.
Critical evaluation: Base rate problems receive surprisingly limited attention in romance fraud detection literature, with many papers reporting impressive sensitivity and specificity without acknowledging that even strong performance may prove practically inadequate. This represents significant omission, as deployment feasibility depends critically on PPV considerations that raw performance metrics obscure.
2.6.2 Privacy, Surveillance, and User Autonomy
Automated romance fraud detection necessitates analysis of intimate communications, raising substantial privacy and autonomy concerns:
Privacy intrusions: Conversation content often includes highly personal information—intimate discussions, personal struggles, sexual content, financial information, health details. Automated analysis, even if performed for protective purposes, constitutes privacy intrusion. Cloud-based analysis exacerbates concerns through centralised data aggregation and potential unauthorised access or misuse.
Surveillance and power asymmetries: Automated detection systems inherently surveil users, creating power asymmetries between monitored users and monitoring entities (platform operators, device manufacturers, governments). Historical experience with surveillance systems demonstrates scope creep, whereby initially narrow applications expand to broader monitoring.
Autonomy and paternalism: Fraud warnings intervene in users' autonomous decision-making regarding relationships. Whilst benevolently motivated, such intervention exhibits paternalistic elements—systems effectively override user judgment. For vulnerable populations already subject to extensive monitoring and reduced autonomy, additional surveillance may prove particularly problematic.
False positive harms: Erroneous fraud accusations carry significant potential for harm—disrupting genuine relationships, causing emotional distress, reinforcing negative stereotypes (particularly regarding foreign partners or cross-cultural relationships), and potentially triggering platform account restrictions.
Mitigation strategies: Several approaches can partially address privacy and autonomy concerns:
On-device processing: Performing detection locally on user devices prevents conversation content from leaving user control, substantially reducing privacy intrusions and surveillance risks. However, this constrains model complexity and prevents learning from population-level patterns.
User control and transparency: Providing users control over whether detection operates, transparency regarding how systems function, and appeals mechanisms for false positives respects user autonomy whilst maintaining protective benefits.
Soft warnings versus interventions: Presenting information and raising questions rather than imposing restrictions preserves user decision-making whilst providing relevant considerations.
Federated learning: Enabling model improvement through aggregated learning across users without centralising raw data mitigates some privacy concerns whilst retaining improvement capabilities.
Critical evaluation: Privacy and autonomy concerns resist purely technical solutions. On-device processing addresses data centralisation but not the fundamental surveillance dynamics. User control mechanisms presume users possess sufficient knowledge to make informed decisions—yet cognitive biases and manipulation inherent to fraud undermine this assumption. The literature inadequately grapples with these tensions, often treating privacy as purely technical challenge addressable through encryption or local processing.
Furthermore, differential impacts across populations receive minimal attention. Systems designed for Western contexts may embed cultural assumptions regarding relationships, communication styles, and privacy norms that prove inappropriate in other contexts. Power differentials mean that marginalised populations may experience greater surveillance and intervention whilst having less influence over system design.
2.6.3 Adversarial Robustness and Adaptation
Deployed detection systems inevitably face adversarial adaptation as perpetrators modify tactics to evade detection:
Obfuscation techniques: Character substitution (m0ney), misspelling, punctuation insertion, and language mixing can defeat keyword-based detection whilst remaining intelligible to humans. Semantic similarity-based detection partially addresses this but may struggle with extensive perturbation.
Strategic content positioning: If systems emphasise recent context, perpetrators may position suspicious content strategically. If systems flag explicit requests, perpetrators may employ increasingly indirect language requiring greater inference.
Mimicry attacks: Perpetrators may study legitimate relationship patterns to better imitate genuine communications, or explicitly study detection systems to identify decision boundaries and craft communications just below detection thresholds.
Arms race dynamics: Detection improvements incentivise tactical adaptation, which necessitates further detection improvements, creating ongoing arms races. Systems lacking adaptation mechanisms rapidly become obsolete.
Critical evaluation: Adversarial considerations receive minimal attention in romance fraud detection literature. Most papers evaluate systems against historical data without considering how performance degrades as perpetrators adapt. This represents significant limitation, as real-world deployment unavoidably triggers adversarial responses.
Furthermore, sophisticated adversaries with access to detection systems (through reverse engineering or insider knowledge) can systematically optimise evasion. The literature inadequately addresses how to design systems maintaining robustness against such adaptive adversaries.
2.7 Critical Synthesis and Research Gaps
2.7.1 Evidence Quality and Generalisability Limitations
The existing literature exhibits several systematic limitations constraining current understanding:
Sampling biases: Most studies analyse confirmed fraud cases, typically from victim support services or law enforcement. This sampling strategy inherently over-represents obvious, unsuccessful frauds whilst missing sophisticated operations evading detection. Indicator prevalence estimates from such samples likely exhibit substantial upward bias.
Comparison group inadequacy: Few studies employ appropriate control groups—matched legitimate relationships exhibiting similar characteristics (online-initiated, long-distance, accelerated development). Without such comparisons, distinguishing fraud-specific patterns from online relationship patterns generally proves difficult.
Cultural and demographic skew: Research concentrates predominantly on Western populations, particularly UK, US, and Australia. Generalisability to non-Western contexts, where communication norms, relationship expectations, and fraud tactics may differ substantially, remains empirically unestablished.
Temporal instability: Fraud tactics evolve. Research from 2010-2015 may inadequately represent contemporary tactics, particularly given accelerating AI integration. Longitudinal studies tracking tactical evolution remain scarce.
Publication bias: Positive findings (indicators that discriminate fraud) likely receive preferential publication over null results (indicators that do not discriminate). This biases literature toward over-optimistic conclusions regarding indicator reliability.
Small sample limitations: Many studies employ modest samples (n<100 fraud cases), limiting statistical power and generalisability. Effect size uncertainty remains substantial but rarely quantified through confidence intervals.
2.7.2 Theoretical Underdevelopment
Romance fraud detection research exhibits limited theoretical development beyond descriptive pattern cataloguing:
Absence of generative theories: Existing models (Whitty's stages, platform affordances) describe what occurs but provide limited explanatory power regarding why particular patterns emerge and under what conditions they vary. This limits ability to predict tactical evolution or generate novel hypotheses.
Disconnection from broader theory: Research rarely integrates insights from relevant theoretical frameworks—social psychology theories of trust and persuasion, criminological theories of confidence tricks, economic theories of information asymmetry, or linguistic theories of pragmatics. This disconnection limits conceptual depth.
Atheoretical indicator selection: Many proposed indicators emerge from descriptive observation rather than theoretical derivation. Without theoretical grounding, indicator sets risk ad hoc character, complicating understanding of why indicators work when they do and when they should fail.
Insufficient mechanism elaboration: How do proposed indicators actually operate? Through what psychological, linguistic, or social mechanisms do they distinguish fraud from legitimate relationships? Mechanism understanding would enable more robust indicator development and adaptation.
2.7.3 Methodological Gaps and Future Directions
Several methodological directions could substantially advance understanding:
Matched case-control studies: Systematic comparison of fraud cases with carefully matched legitimate online relationships would clarify indicator specificity, controlling for online relationship characteristics generally.
Longitudinal observational studies: Following online relationships prospectively (rather than retrospectively) would eliminate hindsight bias and enable understanding of how fraud patterns emerge in real time versus reconstructed retrospectively.
Cross-cultural research: Systematic examination across cultural contexts would identify universal versus culturally specific patterns, essential for systems deployed internationally.
Adversarial robustness evaluation: Systematic testing of detection approaches under simulated adversarial adaptation would clarify vulnerability to tactical evolution.
Intervention studies: Experimental evaluation of different warning presentations, decision aids, and educational interventions would clarify how detection systems can most effectively support users without generating fatigue or restricting autonomy.
Mechanism studies: Research isolating specific psychological and linguistic mechanisms through which indicators operate would strengthen theoretical foundations.
Base rate sensitivity analyses: Systematic examination of how performance metrics translate across different base rate scenarios would clarify deployment feasibility constraints.
2.7.4 Outstanding Research Questions
Several fundamental questions remain inadequately addressed:

Early detection feasibility: Do genuinely early discriminative indicators exist, or do apparently early signals reflect hindsight bias in retrospective annotation? At what point in fraud progression does detection become feasible?
Typological heterogeneity: Do diverse fraud types (direct requests, investment scams, blackmail) require distinct detection approaches, or do unified systems achieve adequate coverage?
False positive impacts: What are actual consequences of false positive warnings on genuine relationships? Under what circumstances do users appropriately vs. inappropriately respond to warnings?
Cultural transferability: Which indicators transfer across cultural contexts versus requiring culturally specific calibration?
Adversarial adaptation rates: How quickly do perpetrators adapt to deployed detection systems, and which system characteristics confer robustness to adaptation?
Optimal intervention design: How should detection systems present findings to maximise protective benefits whilst minimising autonomy intrusions and false positive harms?

2.8 Conclusion
The literature on romance fraud conceptualisation, indicators, and detection reveals a domain characterised by growing empirical attention but persistent methodological limitations and theoretical underdevelopment. Whitty's stage model provides useful organising framework but rests on limited empirical foundation with unclear generalisability. Platform affordances shape fraud dynamics in systematic ways, yet these relationships lack rigorous theoretical elaboration. Numerous conversational indicators demonstrate association with fraud in retrospective analyses, but selection biases, comparison group inadequacies, and base rate neglect limit confidence in their discriminative utility.
Technical approaches leveraging natural language processing and machine learning show promise but face substantial challenges from long-context limitations, adversarial adaptation, and privacy constraints. The literature inadequately addresses fundamental tensions between detection capability and privacy preservation, between sensitivity and false positive rates at realistic base rates, and between static indicator sets and evolving adversarial tactics.
Several critical gaps require attention: appropriate control groups for indicator validation, cross-cultural generalisability assessment, adversarial robustness evaluation, and examination of actual false positive impacts. Most fundamentally, the field requires stronger theoretical development, moving beyond pattern cataloguing toward generative theories that explain why particular patterns emerge and predict how they evolve.
These limitations and gaps establish the context within which the current research proceeds. Rather than assuming literature-derived indicators transfer directly to operational detection systems, empirical validation against appropriate baselines with careful attention to methodological limitations proves essential. The subsequent chapters describe data collection, annotation procedures, and detection methodology designed to address some identified limitations whilst acknowledging constraints on what single studies can accomplish.
